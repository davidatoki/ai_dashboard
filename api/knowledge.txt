URBAN BOTANICAL BIOFILTRATION FOR AIR QUALITY IMPROVEMENT IN A DENSELY POPULATED CITY.
PROBLEM STATEMENT 
Densely populated urban areas worldwide face significant challenges related to air pollution, primarily due to emissions from road transport, biomass burning, and power generation (Bikis, 2023; Vitaliano et al., 2024;). These activities release high levels of particulate matter (PM₂.₅) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs), posing serious public health risks (World Health Organization, 2025). While urban greenery is known to mitigate air pollution and reduce urban heat island effects, many cities lack sufficient and well-maintained green spaces to harness these benefits effectively (Karamveer  et al., 2022; Edeigba et al., 2024). 
Plant–soil biofilters, such as green walls and planters, utilize root–microbe systems to absorb and degrade pollutants like VOCs and particulates. These systems can enhance urban air quality and contribute to climate resilience (Li et al., 2023). However, the effectiveness of native plant species and engineered soil systems in pollutant removal remains under-researched, limiting the design and implementation of green infrastructure that can serve as effective biofilters. Addressing this knowledge gap is crucial for developing sustainable urban ecosystems that support the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities), SDG 13 (Climate Action), and SDG 15 (Life on Land).
RESEARCH QUESTION
In lab-based microcosms simulating Lagos air, how efficiently can selected native plant species filter out VOCs and PM₂.₅? Which combinations of plants amendments maximize VOC absorption and particulate capture, and which rhizosphere microbial communities are responsible for pollutant breakdown?
Objectives are to; 
	•	simulate urban air conditions in laboratory microcosms
	•	assess air purification efficiency of selected plants; and 
	•	analyze rhizosphere microbial communities using metagenomics
 METHODOLOGY
	•	Simulate Urban Air Conditions in Laboratory Microcosms (Hörmann et al., 2017 and Ibrahim et al., 2019)
To replicate urban air pollution scenarios, laboratory microcosms will be established using sealed chambers equipped with controlled ventilation systems. These chambers will be exposed to a mixture of pollutants, including particulate matter (PM₂.₅), volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and ozone, at concentrations mirroring those found in densely populated urban areas. The pollutant levels will be monitored and adjusted using gas generators and particle dispersers to maintain consistent exposure conditions. This setup allows for the controlled study of plant responses to urban air pollution.
	•	Assess Air Purification Efficiency of Selected Plants (Elkamhawy & Jang 2020)
Selected plant species, chosen for their potential in biofiltration, will be cultivated in different engineered soil setups within the microcosms. The air purification efficiency will be evaluated by measuring the reduction in pollutant concentrations within the chamber over time. Gas chromatography will be employed to quantify VOCs, while particle counters will measure PM₂.₅ levels before and after exposure to the plants. This approach provides quantitative data on the plants' ability to filter air pollutants.
	•	Analyze Rhizosphere Microbial Communities Using Metagenomics as shown by (Ezequiel et al., 2024)
Soil samples from the rhizosphere will be collected at various stages of the experiment. DNA will be extracted using standardized kits, and the V3–V4 region of bacterial and archaeal 16S rRNA genes will be amplified using specific primers. The amplicons will be sequenced using high-throughput sequencing platforms, such as Illumina MiSeq. Bioinformatics analysis will be conducted using tools like Mothur and PICRUSt to assess microbial diversity and functional potentials. This methodology allows for a comprehensive understanding of microbial community dynamics and their roles in pollutant degradation. 

EXPECTED OUTCOMES:
	•	A list of plant-soil combinations with high biofiltration efficiency.
	•	Insights into rhizosphere microbial roles in VOC degradation.
	•	Recommendations for urban planning and green infrastructure development.
